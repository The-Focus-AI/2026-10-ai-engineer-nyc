9:00 AM–11:00 AMEvaluations with Phoenix 101Laurie Voss (Arize AI)
About

You shipped an AI agent. It works great on your test queries. But how do you know it works on your users' queries? How do you know a prompt change that fixes one thing didn't break three others? And when a new model drops next quarter, how do you know whether to upgrade? If your answer to any of these is "I run it a few times and check," this workshop is for you. In this hands-on session, you'll build a complete evaluation pipeline for an AI agent from scratch. Starting with a financial analysis chatbot built on the Claude Agent SDK, you'll instrument it with tracing, run it against diverse test cases, and then do the thing most tutorials skip: actually read your data. You'll categorize failures by root cause, define success criteria, and use that analysis to decide what to measure before writing a single eval. Then you'll build three types of evaluators: fast deterministic code checks, built-in LLM-as-a-judge evals like faithfulness, and a custom rubric you design yourself. You'll validate your judges against your own human labels, because an eval you haven't tested is just a fancy way of being wrong at scale. The workshop doesn't stop at scoring. You'll save failure cases as reusable datasets, improve your agent's prompts based on what the evals told you, and run controlled experiments to prove the improvement with numbers instead of gut feel. Along the way, you'll learn practical frameworks: the impact hierarchy for prioritizing fixes, the Swiss Cheese model for layering defenses, and the eval-iterate cycle that turns a prototype into a production system. You'll leave with a working notebook, a free Phoenix Cloud account with your traces and eval results, and a repeatable process you can apply to any AI application the day you get home.

11:05 AM–12:05 PMAI Evaluation 101SallyAnn DeLucia (Arize AI)
About

Building an AI demo is easy. Knowing whether it actually works — and keeping it working in production — is the hard part. Most teams ship agents on vibes: they try a few prompts, the output looks good, and they push to production with no real way to measure quality or catch regressions. This hands-on workshop walks through the full lifecycle of shipping a real AI agent, using a working financial-analyst agent built on the Claude Agent SDK as the running example. You'll instrument it with tracing, do structured error analysis on its actual outputs, and build a layered evaluation suite — from cheap deterministic code checks to LLM-as-a-judge evaluators with custom rubrics. We'll cover the parts most tutorials skip: why agents fail in ways single LLM calls don't, the eval anti-patterns that quietly mislead you, and how to know whether you can even trust your judge (meta-evaluation). Finally, we'll close the loop: turning eval results into datasets and experiments, running evals online against production traffic, wiring them to monitors and alerts, and feeding failure explanations back to a coding agent to actually fix the underlying problems. You'll leave with a runnable notebook and a repeatable, evaluation-driven workflow you can apply to your own agents the next day.

That creates a fundamental problem for how we evaluate increasingly agentic AI systems. Most evals still ask some version of one question: Did the model or agent complete the task correctly? But task success is not system success. An agent can reach the expected answer after retrieving unsupported evidence, selecting the wrong tool, passing invalid arguments, crossing an authority boundary, or taking an action that should never have been available to it. We use Risk-Based Evaluability Design (R-BED) to organize system evaluation around three connected questions: Technical Failure: Where can the system fail? Deployment Harm: Under what conditions can that failure reach people, processes, or consequential decisions? Business Impact: If it does, how broadly and severely can the consequence propagate, and what can be recovered? These questions cannot be collapsed into a single score or task-success metric. A technically weak system may remain contained. A seemingly small technical error may become material because authority, exposure, or controls allow it to propagate. Evaluability is the ability to determine, with evidence, whether a deployed AI system remains within acceptable bounds and continues to serve its intended purpose. For agentic systems, that means making the trajectory observable: the evidence retrieved, sources used, tool selected, arguments passed, state transitions made, authority exercised, human interventions triggered, and actions taken. We then turn those findings into an engineering discipline: BUILD: Define the system claim, operating boundary, evidence requirements, and permitted authority. BREAK: Stress the system, discover failure regions, and trace failures across evidence, execution, tools, workflows, and controls. FIX: Constrain, redesign, route, monitor, or intervene, then verify that the change actually worked. The key insight is that meaningful system evals cannot simply be bolted onto an AI application after it has been built. If the system does not expose the evidence needed to reconstruct what happened, important questions become impossible to answer. Evaluability has to be designed into the system. As AI systems become more autonomous and gain access to tools, workflows, data, and consequential actions, the distance between a technical error and a real-world consequence is shrinking. The next generation of evals therefore cannot stop at asking: “Did the model get the answer right?” They must also ask: Where can the system fail? Can that failure reach the real world?
